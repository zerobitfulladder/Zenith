What We Are Building

A plain-words tour of the system in this folder: how it writes things down, how it remembers, how it looks, how it learns, how it searches, and what it has actually done so far. No equations. Where a number matters it is in a table.

The one-sentence version. Instead of training a network to compute an answer, we keep a memory with strict rules about how things are written and how many things it can hold, and we let learning be nothing more than counting what recurs and giving it a name. Everything else — looking, guessing, checking, having ideas — is what that memory forces you to do.

1. How things are written down: fingerprints

Every thing the system knows — a stroke, a position, a chunk, a label, a tag like "I observed this" — is written as a fingerprint. A fingerprint is a row of switches, mostly off. In the real system it is 656 switches with 16 on; in the pictures below it is 32 switches with 4 on, which behaves the same way.

The switches are arranged in blocks, and the rule is: exactly one switch is on in each block. So a fingerprint is really just "which switch, in each block". You can picture each block as a ring of notches with one notch marked.

Two fingerprints are similar when they mark the same notch in many blocks. Two unrelated fingerprints agree on a block only by chance (1 in 8 here, 1 in 41 in the real system), so strangers overlap almost nowhere and relatives overlap a lot.

Moving a fingerprint: poses

To say "stroke A at position P" we do not write A and P side by side. We walk A's marked notch along each ring by an amount that P dictates: P is itself a fingerprint, and its marked notch in each block is the number of steps to walk. Walking wraps around the ring. The result looks like a brand-new fingerprint — it shares nothing with A and nothing with P — but walking back by the same amounts recovers A exactly.

Two facts make this the heart of the design:

  • Walking keeps relatives related. If A and B agree on three blocks, then A-at-P and B-at-P agree on the same three blocks. So a shape is recognisable wherever it is placed.
  • Two walks add up. Walking by P and then by Q is the same as walking once by P+Q. So "position" and "offset inside a part" combine by the same operation. Moving the whole moves every part correctly for free.

2. Holding many things at once: the pile

Memory needs to hold several fingerprints at the same time. We do that with a pile: a counter behind every switch. Adding an item adds 1 to its on-switches. Removing it subtracts 1. That is all.

To ask "is item X in the pile?", look at X's on-switches: if every one of them has a count above zero, X is there. With a handful of items this is exact. But the pile has a limit: as more items go in, more counters are non-zero, and eventually some fingerprint that was never added has all its switches covered by other items' switches. The pile then says "yes" to a ghost.

This is not a bug to be engineered away; it is the whole reason the system behaves the way it does. In the real system the pile holds about 20 items before ghosts appear. That is the size of its working memory, and it forces everything downstream: it cannot hold a whole image as separate parts, so it must look at a few things, compress what it recognises into one item, and predict the rest.

3. The table: what the system knows for good

Long-term memory is a stack of cards. Every card has a fingerprint (its name) and, if it is a made thing, a list of parts at offsets:

  • Primitive cards — the alphabet. In the toy world these are given. On MNIST they are stroke prototypes the system allocated itself: small image patches, kept only if a patch like that kept coming back.
  • Chunk cards — a group of parts at fixed offsets from each other, with no position of its own. A part can be a primitive or another chunk, so cards nest. "The loop of a 6" could be a chunk of chunks of strokes.
  • Exemplar / class cards — a whole thing with a label attached: a remembered digit, or (in the counting version) a class's table of what tends to sit where.

Every made card starts provisional — cheap to write, allowed to be wrong, allowed to be forgotten. It becomes consolidated only by earning it. That earning step is the "click" (section 6).

Why parts are stored relative to each other. A chunk card says "stroke 12, and stroke 48 one cell to its right, and stroke 9 two cells below". It does not say where on the canvas. When the chunk is recognised in a picture it gets an anchor — the canvas position of its first cell — and then it is one ordinary item, "chunk C at anchor (4,7)". The same card serves every position.

4. Working memory: one pile with tags

During an episode (one object, one digit) everything the system currently believes sits in a single pile. Each item is tag ⊗ name ⊗ position, where the tag says where the belief came from:

tagmeaningwho writes it
observedI looked here and saw thisthe sensor
inferredI recognised these parts as this chunkrecognition (compaction)
assumedI am currently entertaining this hypothesisthe guessing step

Because the tag is part of the fingerprint, "what have I actually seen?" and "what am I only assuming?" are the same kind of question as "what is at cell 12?": walk the pile back by the tag and read what lines up. The system reads its own beliefs through the same operation it uses for everything else, ghosts included.

5. The loop: one glimpse at a time

The system never sees a whole picture. It sees one cell per glimpse. Between glimpses it runs this loop:

Read the pile. What strokes have I seen where? What have I already inferred?
↓
Vote. Every stroke I have seen votes for every card that has a similar stroke at a compatible position. Each card, placed at each possible anchor, gets a score: how many cells agree, how many disagree. (Cards that disagree with something seen are out.)
↓
Compress if you can. If a chunk's cells are all seen and agree, replace those strokes in the pile by the single chunk item. Fewer items, more room. Recognition and compression are the same act.
↓
Keep a few guesses in mind. The strongest hypotheses go into the pile as "assumed" items — only a handful, because the pile is small. One slot is always reserved for the best idea (a provisional card), so ideas get tested instead of crowded out.
↓
Under pressure? If the pile is getting full: keep fewer guesses; then try to compress what is seen into a new provisional chunk; and if that still is not enough, stop looking — overwhelmed.
↓
Decide or look. Declare only when one hypothesis clearly dominates, explains everything seen, has evidence in more than one of its parts, and has made predictions about unseen cells that came true. Otherwise choose the next cell: where the guesses disagree most, or, if one guess leads alone, the cell where it is most exposed (a part of it with no evidence yet, far from what was seen).

The "where to look" rule matters a lot. On the toy world, looking where hypotheses disagree recognised objects in about 6 glimpses with no mistakes, while just exploring the frontier fell to 23% once the library was rich. On MNIST it saved about 1.4 glimpses per digit and no more; digits are less about pinpoint differences.

6. Learning: counting, naming, clicking

There are no weights and no error signal anywhere. Every piece of learning is one of three things.

6a. Counting

When the label is shown (the MNIST counting experiments), the system keeps a table: for class 7, at cell 12, how often each stroke appeared, and how often the cell was blank. That is the whole "model" of a class. Reading it back gives "how expected is this stroke here for a 7", and multiplying those over the cells you have seen gives an opinion about the class. Surprise is just how unexpected what you saw was. Pairs are counted too: "this item and that item, at this relative offset, occurred together".

6b. Naming what recurs: ideas

A new card appears when something recurs. Three ways:

  • A pair recurs. Two items at the same relative offset seen together enough times become a provisional chunk. Chunks are items too, so pairs of chunks make bigger chunks, level by level.
  • A shared sub-pattern is noticed. Part of what I see now is also part of something I remember, and that shared part shows up in at least two independent things. That is an idea: name the shared part. (This is how the toy world recovered its hidden sub-assemblies exactly.)
  • Pressure. The pile is full and nothing known explains the scene: group what is seen into a new provisional chunk and collapse it. A guess, to be tested later.

6c. The click, and its opposite

A provisional card is written for good — it clicks — when two things are true:

  1. It pays for itself. Think of description length as "how many yes/no questions would I need to write all of this down". A card costs questions to store. It saves questions every time it stands in for several items: as one item in a scene, or as one part inside another card. When the questions saved exceed the questions spent, the card has paid for itself. Nobody sets a threshold; the arithmetic decides.
  2. It has worked. It was used as a hypothesis and predicted an unseen cell correctly at least once. An idea is a compaction the system tries; the click is the verdict after the test.

The opposite happens too: a consolidated card whose savings evaporate (because a better card made its parents cheaper another way) is inlined back into its parents and forgotten. Same arithmetic, other direction.

The lesson we learned the hard way. The first version credited a chunk every time it was recognised, including inside a parent that was itself a card. That is fictitious: a fragment that only ever occurs inside one chunk saves nothing. Eight junk two-cell fragments consolidated. Counting savings structurally — once per scene it stands for alone, once per distinct card it sits inside — fixed it: zero junk afterwards.

6d. Usefulness, not frequency

Naming what recurs most gives you what every digit shares — a little cross, a bar — which tells you nothing about which digit it is. In the counting experiments that library hurt accuracy by 13 points. So the newer versions score a chunk by what it does: every time it is placed with some cells unseen, it predicts them, and when those cells arrive the prediction is scored in bits against the class table. Its label value is scored the same way. A chunk earns by removing surprise, not by being common.

7. Search: where the "OMG" comes from

Counting pairs only finds things that sit next to each other. It cannot discover that three strokes far apart, plus the label, go together. For that there is a small bounded search, run only when the current digit is still surprising after everything known has been applied — search when unexplained, not always.

Start from each seen stroke as a one-item candidate.
↓
Extend each candidate by a few more strokes. Which ones first? The ones the pair counts say have co-occurred with it most (the heuristic), plus one random one — the creative slot, the only way a genuinely new combination can enter.
↓
Score each candidate: among recent digits that contained this combination, how strongly did it point to the current label? Keep the best few (the beam), extend again, a few levels deep.
↓
If the best candidate explains a lot at once — "these three things are why this is a 7" — write it as a provisional card. Then it must earn its click like any other.

Honest status: the search is built and runs, but so far it has produced almost nothing, because its memory of recent digits was keyed by exact stroke identity, so a three-stroke combination rarely had three past digits in common. The newest version keys it by stroke families instead. Not yet measured.

8. The latest version: integrate as you go

This is the design from our last conversation. Cells arrive one at a time in random order, nearly the whole digit. After every cell the system may reorganise what it holds:

  • the new stroke votes for chunks; a chunk with at least half its cells present and none contradicted may be placed;
  • a placement may take cells that an existing chunk owns if it explains more; the displaced chunk dissolves back into its strokes; this repeats until nothing changes — so having grouped 1 and 2, when 3 arrives it can regroup 1 with 3 if that fits better, or build a chunk-of-chunks on top;
  • at most 20 top-level items. Over the cap it must compact, and if it cannot, it drops its oldest stroke. The opinion about the class forms from what is held, not from what was shown;
  • every placed chunk predicts its unseen cells, and when they arrive the prediction is scored — that is the natural, online test of a chunk.

At the end it is asked the class, then asked to predict a few held-out cells, answering from the parse (the chunk covering the cell) before falling back to the class table.

Runs correctly on 300 digits: the library answers about a third of held-out cells and improves completion. The full 5,000-digit run stalled: under memory pressure it minted a new provisional chunk almost every time it went over the cap, reached 5,000 provisional chunks after 300 digits, and the matching step drowned. The fix is a gate on pressure-born chunks; not applied yet.

10. What is honestly still missing

  • The primitive codes are made by counting and clustering, not learned end to end; and the fixed patch grid has no translation invariance below a cell.
  • Poses compose exactly for translations only. Rotation is an operator applied outside the fingerprint arithmetic.
  • Search has not yet found anything worth keeping.
  • The library helps the self-supervised task (predict what you have not seen) more than the label. That may be the honest shape of this idea: it is a memory organiser, not a classifier.
  • Metacognition exists only as a small table of "situations where declaring went wrong". No language, no instruction.
