Where this is going. In this project a fingerprint is a list of angles — one angle per block. A pile is several fingerprints superposed. Two questions decide how the whole architecture behaves: how do you superpose angles, and how many separate opinions can one block hold at once. Both are Fourier questions, and by the end of this page you will be able to answer them yourself.

1. A number that wraps

Start with the most ordinary thing: a clock. Three hours after 11 o'clock is 2 o'clock, not 14. The numbers run out and start again. That is wrapping, and a set of numbers that wraps is a ring.

Our blocks are rings with 41 positions. Position 40 plus 3 lands on position 2. That is the whole of it. But it is easier to think about if you stop drawing the positions in a line and draw them around a circle — because then "wrapping" stops being a special rule and becomes just "going all the way round".

Once the positions are on a circle, a position is an angle. Position 0 is at the right, and each step counter-clockwise advances by one notch, which is 2π/41 of a full turn. So a block's value is an angle, and the 41 notches are just where we happened to put tick marks. Nothing stops us from using the angles in between — that is exactly what "continuous blocks" means.

Widget 1 — the ring

Drag the dot. Watch the angle, the turn fraction and the nearest notch. Notice that the notches are a choice, and the angle underneath is continuous.

Adding angles: walking

Add two angles by adding the numbers and wrapping. On the circle this is rotation: start at angle a, turn by b, land at a+b. The project calls this walking, and it is what binding does to each block.

Two properties come free and both matter enormously:

  • Order does not matter. Turning by b then c is the same as c then b. This is why a card's name built from its parts does not depend on the order the parts were integrated (product.py).
  • Everything is undoable. To undo a turn of b, turn by −b. Exactly, with no loss. This is why "subtract what I have already seen" is one operation and not a mechanism that had to be invented.

Widget 2 — walking, and why it scrambles

Take one step size and keep applying it. If the step divides the circle evenly you land on a few positions forever. If it does not, you scatter all over. This is the only reason a random walk acts like a random rotation: not magic, just wrapping.

Why 41 and why prime. 41 is prime, so every non-zero step size eventually visits all 41 positions before repeating — there are no small cycles to get stuck in. It also means any repeated walk can be divided back out, which is what lets a card built from K parts be shifted by t (it moves by K·t) and then un-shifted.

2. Arrows, not angles

Here is the problem that forces everything else. Suppose three voters give opinions about one block: 10°, 20°, 30°. The average is 20°, fine. Now suppose they say 350°, 10°, 30°. The numerical average is 130° — which is nowhere near any of them. The arithmetic broke, because the numbers wrap and averaging does not know that.

You cannot average angles. You can average arrows.

Write an angle θ as an arrow of length 1 pointing that way: (cos θ, sin θ). Now "add the opinions" means putting the arrows nose to tail and seeing where you end up. This always works, never wraps wrongly, and it hands you something extra for free:

direction of the sum = the consensus angle length of the sum = how much they agreed (0 = no agreement, 1 = perfect agreement)

That second line is not an extra feature somebody designed in. It is the leftover when unit arrows point different ways. A pile block is therefore two numbers, not one: an angle and a length. The length is the confidence, and it arrives whether you want it or not.

Keep this arrow in mind. It has a proper name that we will not need until Part 7, and when it turns up there it will not be a new object — it will be this one.

Widget 3 — adding arrows

Left: the arrows laid nose to tail, with the resultant in orange. Right: the same items as points on the ring. Try the presets. Watch "two opposite" carefully — the sum is zero, meaning "no opinion", even though the voters were not confused at all. They were split. A single arrow cannot tell those apart, and that limitation is the reason Part 5 exists.

Capacity, in one sentence. N arrows pointing in random directions sum to a length of about 1/√N — so a pile of 20 unrelated items leaves any single one at about a quarter strength. That √N law is the capacity limit the design keeps running into, and you can watch it happen in the widget by raising "items" with spread at maximum.

3. Many rings at once — the torus

One block is one ring. A fingerprint is 16 blocks, so it is 16 angles: one point on each of 16 independent rings. The name for "several independent rings considered together" is a torus.

Two rings make the familiar doughnut. Pick an angle around the big loop and an angle around the tube, and you have named a point on the surface. The important word is independent: moving around the tube does not change where you are around the big loop. Our 16 blocks are independent in exactly that way, which is why a code can satisfy 16 different constraints at once — block 3 can carry "this predicts a 7" while block 9 carries "this sits next to a bar", and they do not fight.

Widget 4 — two rings are a doughnut

Drag either dial. The square is the same space cut open and flattened — when the point leaves the right edge it comes back on the left, because the edges are glued. The doughnut is that square rolled up so the gluing is real.

Binding, on the torus

To bind two fingerprints, add them ring by ring. Block 1 of the first plus block 1 of the second, block 2 plus block 2, and so on. Every block wraps on its own. Because each ring obeys the two properties from Part 1, so does the whole fingerprint: order does not matter, and it undoes exactly.

Widget 5 — binding is walking every ring

Eight blocks. Blue is a stroke's code, violet is a pose, orange is the two bound together. Reroll and see that the result looks unrelated to either — and then press unbind the pose and watch the blue come back exactly.

Holographic, since you asked yesterday. Notice the bound code has no particular block "holding" the stroke and no block "holding" the pose — every block carries a little of both. That is what holographic means: the information is smeared over the whole thing rather than filed in one place. Lose some blocks and you still recover the item, just less certainly — like cutting a hologram in half and getting the whole picture, dimmer.

4. A ring can hold a crowd

So far each block held one angle. But a pile is many items at once, so what a block really holds is a weight at every position around the ring — a histogram wrapped into a circle. Three voters at 20° and one at 200° is a tall bar at 20° and a short one at 200°.

This is the honest picture of a block, and everything from here is about how to describe such a ring-shaped pattern cheaply.

Widget 6 — a distribution on a ring

Click anywhere on or near the circle to drop weight there; click again to add more. The bars stick out from the circle. This is what a block is actually holding when several things are piled into it.

The single arrow of Part 2 is one way to summarise this histogram: it is the histogram's centre of mass. Cheap — two numbers instead of 41 — but lossy. Load the "two spikes" preset and watch the arrow collapse to nearly nothing, the same as if you had loaded "scatter". Two completely different situations, one summary.

The fix is to summarise the histogram with more than one number-pair. Which pairs? That is Fourier's answer, and we can now build it.

5. Waves that fit — what a harmonic is

Forget piles for a moment. Here is a pure question: what are the natural shapes on a circle?

On a circle, a pattern must join up with itself — go all the way round and you are back where you started, so the value has to match. The simplest patterns with that property are waves that fit a whole number of times around:

  • k = 0 — flat. The same everywhere. "How much is there in total."
  • k = 1 — one hump and one dip. High on one side, low on the other. "Which side."
  • k = 2 — two humps opposite each other. "Is it split across a diameter."
  • k = 3 — three humps. And so on.

A wave that fits k whole times around is called the k-th harmonic. The word comes from music: pluck a string and it vibrates at one main frequency plus whole-number multiples of it, and those multiples are the harmonics that give the note its character. Same arithmetic here, bent into a circle. k is sometimes called the frequency: how many cycles per trip round.

Widget 7 — the harmonics

Left: the wave drawn as a deformation of the circle (pushed out where it is positive, pulled in where negative). Right: the same wave unrolled into a flat line, which is the picture people usually draw. The phase slider slides the wave around the ring.

Fix the picture in your head now, because everything depends on it: harmonic k answers one specific question about a ring-shaped pattern — "how much does this pattern look like k humps arranged evenly around the circle, and where are those humps?" k = 1 is "which side is it on". k = 2 is "is it two-sided".

6. Every pattern is a pile of waves

Here is the claim that makes Fourier useful, and it is a genuinely surprising fact rather than a definition:

Any pattern on a circle can be written as a sum of harmonics. Give each harmonic an amount (how tall) and a phase (where its humps sit), add them all up, and you can reproduce any ring-shaped pattern exactly.

Amount and phase together are just... an arrow. Length = amount, direction = phase. So the recipe for a pattern on a ring is one arrow per harmonic. Those arrows are the Fourier coefficients, usually written c0, c1, c2, ….

Two directions, and both have names that sound harder than they are:

DirectionNameIn words
pattern → arrowsFourier transform (or analysis)measure how much of each harmonic is in there
arrows → patterninverse transform (or synthesis)add the harmonics back up

That is all "taking a Fourier transform" means: measuring the harmonic content. It is a change of description, not a change of the thing. Same pattern, listed by "how much of each wave" instead of by "how much at each position".

Widget 8 — build a shape out of waves

Drag the amount sliders to add harmonics together and watch the shape form. Then press a target button: the page measures that target's harmonics and sets the sliders for you — that is the Fourier transform, done to something in front of you. Note how few harmonics a smooth bump needs and how many a sharp spike needs.

7. Measuring one wave — the k-th coefficient

How do you actually measure "how much of harmonic k is in this pattern"? The operation is so simple it is almost disappointing, and it is exactly what our code does.

c_k = sum over items j of (weight_j) × (arrow pointing at k × θ_j)

In words: take every item, spin its angle k times faster, and add up the arrows.

Why does spinning k times faster measure harmonic k? Because if the items really are arranged in k humps, multiplying their angles by k brings all those humps around to the same place, so the arrows line up and the sum is long. If they are not arranged that way, the spun arrows point every which way and cancel to nothing.

You have already computed one of these. Put k = 1 in that formula. Spinning every angle by 1 leaves it alone, so it reads: take every item and add up the arrows — which is exactly what you did in Part 2. The pile's arrow is c1. It always was; we simply had no name for it yet.

This is worth being precise about, because it is easy to get backwards. Fourier is not something you do to the pile afterwards. The pile's arrow is already the first member of a family, and what Fourier adds is the rest of the family: the same add-up-the-arrows move, done with every angle spun twice, three times, and so on. It tells you what the next-cheapest summaries are, instead of leaving you to invent extra numbers by guesswork.

If you have seen eiθ before: that is just notation for "the unit arrow at angle θ", and c_k = Σj wj e−ikθj is the line above written compactly. The minus sign is a bookkeeping convention about which way you spin. Nothing new is happening.

Widget 9 — spin by k and add

Top: your items on the ring. Bottom: each item's arrow after spinning k times, and their sum in orange. Move k and watch the sum grow and shrink. The demonstration that matters: load "two opposite", then step k from 1 to 2. At k = 1 the arrows cancel — that is the "no opinion" reading. At k = 2 they line up perfectly. The second coefficient sees a split that the first one is blind to.

This is the answer to "how many opinions can one block hold". Keep only c1 and a block can hold one opinion plus a vagueness. Keep c1 and c2 and it can tell "torn between two things" from "no idea". Keep more and it can hold more. The design record's Part X.4 says ambiguity should be carried as superposition rather than resolved too early — the number of coefficients you keep is literally how much superposition a block is capable of.

8. Keeping only a few — truncation and blur

A ring of 41 positions needs about 20 arrows to be described exactly. Keeping fewer is called truncating, and it has a very specific effect: you get the pattern back, blurred.

The letter m, once and for all. m is simply how many coefficients you keep per block. Nothing more.
m = 1one arrow. One opinion plus a vagueness. This is what the project actually runs.
m = 2enough to tell "split between two things" from "no idea".
m ≈ 20the exact 41-slot histogram — the original substrate, recovered.

The reason is that high harmonics are the wiggly ones. Harmonic 1 varies slowly around the circle; harmonic 15 wiggles fifteen times. Fine detail lives in the high harmonics, so dropping them drops the detail and keeps the broad shape. Keep the first m and the finest thing you can still see is about 1/m of the way round the circle.

Widget 10 — the blur dial

Grey: the true histogram. Blue: what you get back from the first m coefficients. Slide m from 1 upward. At m = 1 everything is a single blob — load "two spikes" and you will see the two merge into one lump in the wrong place. That is the failure mode a single arrow has.

Two different "more", easy to confuse. Both sound like "give the system more room", and in our measurements they came out opposite ways.
more blocksmore coefficients (m)
what it addsmore independent rings, each with its own single opinion finer resolution inside one ring, so it can hold two opinions at once
costsstored parameters, one angle per block per codenothing stored — only the working pile gets bigger
measured16 → 128 blocks: 38% → 64% m = 1 → 8: 38% → 24%
The free knob is the one that lost. The likely reason is not that a block cannot use the extra room, but that the extra harmonics make the learning problem rough — harmonic k wiggles k times as fast, so its slope flips sign k times as often, and the search gets lost. A block's inability to hold two opinions is real; it was just not what was hurting us.
One knob between two substrates. Our original block stored 41 slot counts. The angle version stores one arrow. Those are not two different designs — they are m = 20 and m = 1 of the same design. Everything in between is available, and it costs nothing in stored parameters, because the codes stay one angle per block; only the working pile carries the extra arrows.

Squinting, and what it does and does not clean up

Throwing away the high coefficients is a low-pass filter — it keeps the broad shape and discards the fine wiggle. That sounds like noise removal, and sometimes it is. Whether it helps depends entirely on where the noise lives, and our two kinds sit in opposite places.

kind of noisewhere it landsdoes low-passing help?
position error — an item sits a notch off from where you expected harmonic k gets a phase error of k·ε, so the 8th is eight times as wrong as the 1st yes, a lot — the damage is concentrated exactly where you are cutting
crosstalk — the other items in the pile interfering every item is a spike, and a spike has every harmonic at full strength, so crosstalk is flat across k no, it hurts — signal is flat too, so you discard both equally and are left with fewer measurements

Crosstalk is the noise a pile actually suffers from, so "squinting removes noise" does not transfer there. But the position row matters, and it lands somewhere useful: m = 1 is already maximally low-passed. The phase error in c1 is 1·ε, the smallest it can be. So the setting the project runs is also the most position-robust setting available — an independent reason for m = 1, alongside the measured one.

Two different things get called squinting, worth keeping apart. Dropping harmonics makes each ring blurrier about position. Using only some of the blocks — 4 of 16 for a cheap first pass, then all 16 — makes the code coarser about identity. The design record means the second one, and it is still unused.

9. Shifting is spinning — why binding and Fourier agree

Now the fact that makes all of this more than a nice way to draw things.

Binding shifts a block's whole pattern around the ring. If every item walks by the same amount t, the histogram rotates by t. What happens to the coefficients?

shift the pattern by t → every arrow c_k spins by k·t and NO arrow changes length

The lengths are untouched. Only the directions move — and the k-th one moves k times as fast, because harmonic k goes round k times per trip.

Widget 11 — shift the pattern, watch the arrows spin

Drag the shift. Top: the histogram rotating. Bottom: its first five coefficient arrows. Watch c3 go round three times while c1 goes round once, and watch every length stay frozen.

Three consequences, all of which the project already ran into:

  • "Binding is linear after all." In the slot picture, binding looked like a weird combinatorial shuffle. In the arrow picture it is multiplication by a fixed arrow, per block. Same operation, and the second description is the one you can do calculus on.
  • A card knows how many leaves it has. Bind K things and shift them all by t, and the card's own arrows spin by K·t. The leaf count is readable from how fast the name moves. Above 40 leaves it wraps past itself and the count becomes ambiguous — which is the limit product.py measured.
  • The lengths are a shift-proof signature. Anything computed from the lengths alone does not care where the object sits. That is translation invariance, available for free, and not yet used anywhere in the codebase.

10. Smearing, and whether you can undo it

Last piece. So far binding moved everything by one exact amount. What if instead of "shift by exactly t" you want "shift by roughly t" — a soft, graded pose? Then each item spreads into a little bump instead of landing on a point. Spreading every item by the same bump and adding up the results is called convolution. It is just controlled smearing.

In the arrow description, convolution becomes almost trivially simple:

smear a pattern with a bump → multiply each c_k by the bump's own c_k

Every harmonic is scaled by however much of that harmonic the bump contains. Which immediately tells you when the smearing can be undone: divide. And division works fine — unless you are dividing by zero.

A smear is undoable exactly when none of its coefficients is zero. A sharp point has every coefficient at full length, so exact binding always undoes. A narrow bump has small but non-zero coefficients, so it undoes in principle but amplifies noise where they are smallest. A wide bump genuinely kills some harmonics, and what is killed is gone. That is why the record says graded binding is "invertible exactly when no wave amplitude is zero", and why the recommendation is to keep the product exact and let graded similarity act only in cleanup.

If you want to go further

You now have the working half of a subject that does go deep. Reasonable next stops, in order of how much they would pay us back:

  1. Why the harmonics are the right shapes — they are the patterns that shifting cannot mix up (shift a harmonic and you get the same harmonic, spun). That "unmixable under shifting" property is the real definition, and everything else follows from it.
  2. The uncertainty trade — narrow in position means spread out in harmonics, and the other way round. A sharp spike needs every harmonic; a wide smooth bump needs a few. You already saw this in Widget 8.
  3. The FFT — a way of computing all the coefficients at once in n log n steps instead of n². Purely an efficiency trick; it changes nothing conceptual.

Nothing beyond those three is needed for anything in this project.
